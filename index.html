⭐ Welcome to Health Decoded ⭐

Welcome to Health Decoded, a student-led health education platform where curiosity meets science. Explore the reasons behind everyday health experiences, discover the truth about common health myths, and learn more about the world of healthcare through engaging, evidence-based content.

Our Mission

Health Decoded is a digital health education blog created to make healthcare more engaging, understandable, and relevant for youth. Through curiosity-driven articles, evidence-based explanations, personal health experiments, monthly challenges, and educational resources, Health Decoded helps young people explore the science behind their everyday health.

The goal is to transform healthcare from something complicated or intimidating into something approachable and interesting.

MYSTERY DECODED • CASE 002

🔍 Why Does Stress Make You Feel Sick?

September 1, 2026

Introduction

Have you ever felt nauseous or had a racing heart before an important exam, event, or presentation? This happens because your brain and body are constantly communicating.

Stress can affect much more than how we feel emotionally. It can create real physical changes throughout the body.

What Is Stress?

Stress is a natural response from the body when faced with a challenge or situation called a stressor.

When the brain senses stress, it activates the stress response, which prepares the body to react by changing activity in the nervous system, hormones, and other body systems.

Short-term stress can help a person remain focused and alert. However, stress that continues for a long time can affect physical and mental well-being.

What's Happening in Your Body?

Your Brain & Nervous System

When the brain detects stress, it activates the sympathetic nervous system, commonly associated with the body's fight-or-flight response.

This system helps control functions such as heart rate, breathing, and alertness.

When sympathetic nervous system activity increases, heart rate and breathing can increase and muscles can become tense.

Your Brain & Nervous System

The nervous system allows the brain to communicate with the rest of the body. During a stress response, signals from the brain can influence heart rate, breathing, muscles, digestion, and other functions.

Your HPA Axis & Cortisol

The HPA axis is a communication system involving the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and adrenal glands.

These structures help regulate the release of cortisol, a hormone involved in the body's response to stress.

Cortisol also plays roles in energy regulation and metabolism.

Your Muscles

Stress can cause muscle tension. If stress continues for a long time, that tension may contribute to headaches, muscle aches, and jaw tension.

Your Stomach

The brain communicates with the digestive system through the gut-brain axis, a two-way communication network between the brain and gastrointestinal system.

Because of this connection, stress can affect digestion and may contribute to symptoms such as nausea, stomach discomfort, diarrhea, constipation, or changes in appetite.

Your Sleep

Stress can make it difficult for the brain and body to relax, which can interfere with falling asleep or staying asleep.

Poor sleep can also make managing stress more difficult, creating a cycle that can be difficult to break.

The Evidence Says…

Organizations including the National Institutes of Health, National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, and American Psychological Association have examined connections between stress and physical and mental health.

Research shows that stress can affect multiple systems of the body, including the nervous, digestive, cardiovascular, and immune systems.

Not every physical symptom is caused by stress. If symptoms persist or interfere with everyday life, it is important to talk with a healthcare professional.

Quick Fun Facts

  • Stress can cause hiccups.
  • Researchers are still studying stress-related yawning.
  • Your pupils can dilate during stress.

What Should You Remember?

Stress can produce physical reactions because the brain and body communicate closely. Short-term stress is a normal response, while long-lasting stress can affect several body systems.

  • National Institute of Mental Health — I'm So Stressed Out!
  • NCCIH — Relaxation Techniques
